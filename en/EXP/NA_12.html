Who Wants to Trade With You
A Conversation About Skill, Risk and Position

Thinking Risk Career
2026.09.06 BY GHOST_1010

I spent twenty minutes this morning talking with a friend who runs his own small business. It started from something I said: he pays me, so ideally I should be able to turn those resources into more money. His reply split that sentence into three layers — an employee's position cannot make that trade, the real gate on trading skill for money is who wants to trade with you, and the trade itself is the risk. This is the chain of reasoning, and what it changed about my definition of being the boss.

# It starts with a sentence that sounds reasonable

What I said came out roughly as: since you are paying, ideally I should be able to turn those resources into more money. That sentence had held together in my head for a long time — I have skill, the resources are here, multiply them and more money should come out.

His answer was one line.

"While you are still an employee, that trade is impossible."

The condition he added: the day you are out in the world working for yourself, the day you are your own boss, the trade becomes possible. And at that moment his own role changes too — he said that by then he would be an investor.

Same person, same money, two different things depending on the relationship. A salary buys output. An investment buys a share of the future. What decides which one it is happens to be the position I stand in.

# The gate sits on the demand side

He then took "trading skill for money" apart.

"Trading skill for money. You have skill. So here comes the question: who wants to trade with you."

Skill belongs to the supply side. It can be trained, measured, and grown by putting hours in. Demand is a separate thing, decided by other people, and I hold no control over it.

I had been treating the trade as a function of skill, where more input yields more output. His version has two independent conditions: whether I hold something tradeable, and whether anyone out there wants it right now. When the second one is zero, the first one multiplies into nothing.

# The middle part is the risk

My reaction at the time: the process of getting to the money is the risk. He agreed.

One step further gets you the difference between an employee and a boss. An employee hands over skill and the money is certain, with an amount and a date already promised. A boss hands over skill and the money is a probability, separated by a stretch of time, a negotiation, and a counterparty who might walk.

That stretch of time is the risk itself. He said he is the one carrying it.

That line explains the first one. An employee's position cannot reach that multiple, because the multiple is priced off carrying the stretch. No carrying, no payment for it.

# What carrying the risk looks like day to day

I asked what the job actually looks like. All three of his answers were mundane.

"Pie in the sky, lie flavoured, served every single day."

"Sometimes I have to supply emotional value before the money moves."

"They cannot even say what they want, and I still have to build it for them."

All three point at the same structure: the demand side hands over no spec. Someone arrives with a vague expectation, and turning it into something deliverable is done by the person carrying the risk.

Skill covers part of that. Sitting through the promises, supplying the emotional labour, and starting work while the information is still thin all count as the same job.

# The opening

I asked whether that means starting a business is off the table for me, then answered it myself: making money needs an opening in your principles wide enough to hold risk, and that opening is where the possibility lives.

He said yes.

The opening means your value system keeps room for income that fluctuates, for things that stay unresolved, for being let down over a stretch of time. With that room in place, risk has somewhere to sit. Its size gets decided by temperament and by how much you can absorb.

Someone can hold strong skill and a narrow opening, which puts their path inside an employee's position, and that combination stands on its own.

# What the conversation changed

My picture of being the boss used to lean on resources and scale: money in hand, people, projects. After this conversation it reads as one sentence — the boss is the person who takes uncertainty off everyone else. How much uncertainty you can absorb sets how big the thing gets.

I am in an employee's position. It buys certain income, and it costs the multiple. That is a trade with a printed price, and I can keep choosing it, or work on widening the opening some day.

For today, seeing the pricing clearly is enough.
