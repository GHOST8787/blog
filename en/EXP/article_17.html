AI Looks Real Because It Looks Like the Past
New Things Only Appear in Execution

Thinking AI Education
2026.10.31 BY GHOST_1010

AI output looks real. Every sentence it produces comes out of probability, and that probability comes from how people have spoken, written, and acted in the past. Put plainly: what AI is best at giving you is the past. This piece has three parts: why AI naturally stands in the past, where new things come from, and who stays in the end.

# AI Gives You the Past

When AI answers, it uses existing data to predict "what a person is most likely to say next at this point." Reports, code, emails: it can hand you a complete answer fast, and it usually reads well.

A huge amount of past data has already told it how people tend to write, which sentences sound reasonable, and which structures are common. The most common way of writing reads smoothly, and it is easy to take it for the right one.

Smooth and right still sit some distance apart.

In 2025, METR ran a study in which experienced developers used the AI tools of the time on open-source projects they knew well. Under those specific conditions, the developers expected to be about 24% faster beforehand, still felt about 20% faster afterward, and actually took about 19% longer.

The problem may lie in how its output looks too much like a reasonable answer, so people underestimate the cost of checking, revising, and integrating it. AI looks like the past done right, and people easily mistake "looks reasonable" for "actually solved."

Why AI appeared is easy to understand. Time is one of the scarcest resources people have. With limited time, people naturally want to do more in less of it; yet what one person can read, hear, remember, and actually do all have limits.

So people have always looked for ways to preserve others' experience and pass it to the next person. Books do this, education does this, databases do this, and now AI does too.

If you see AI as a tool that compresses a huge amount of human experience and serves it back to you, it resembles a modern elixir: swallow it and you get what others took years to build. What sits inside the elixir is still what has already happened.

# New Things Only Appear in Execution

Execution always runs into difficulty. And difficulty is usually where the present differs from the past. If the old methods fully applied, nothing would get stuck; getting stuck means a situation old experience never handled has shown up.

This is also where AI is relatively weak. AI predicts from the past, and genuinely new problems sit exactly where the past has no complete answer.

Someone runs into the problem and solves it. That solution then becomes a new "past": the next person can learn it, and the next model can absorb it. That is how society moves forward, bit by bit. New things are forced out where the answers run short.

Hitting a problem also means you have actually touched the details. People who have not done the work usually see only the overall shape; people who have know which screw comes loose, which step fails most often, and how a small-looking problem can set off a chain reaction later.

So execution and thinking are two sides of the same thing. Think it through and you can carry it out; carry it out and you find where you had not thought it through.

Looking back at the people around me who ask the right questions early, I think the reasons come in three kinds. Heard it: someone told them this could go wrong. Seen it: they watched someone else go wrong here. Done it: they went wrong here themselves.

All three help a person spot problems early; only the source differs. AI can quickly compress "heard it" and part of "seen it" for you, but "done it" is hard to hand over. The real details only show up after you hit the problem and deal with it yourself. That part still has to be paid for in time.

# The People Who Stay

Given the same piece of AI-written work, some accept all of it; some check plenty of sources, but in places that do not matter; a few know which step, once wrong, drags everything after it down. They stop only at those key points, pick out the problem, and fix it.

I think the real gap lies with that last group. Their edge is knowing what cannot be trusted at face value. That ability comes from the previous section: what you have heard, seen, and done, and where you have hit a wall before.

AI makes "not thinking" cheap. In the past, even the laziest person still had to pass through some steps; now, without thinking, checking, or verifying, what you hand in may look as complete as careful work. The real cost is deferred to the day something breaks.

So I think the people most likely to be phased out in the AI era are those who do not think, do not execute, and produce nothing. Handing over AI output as is takes one button, and anyone can press it.

What has real value is knowing which parts can go to AI, which parts need your own judgment, and which parts you have to do yourself.

Apply the same lens to academic degrees and an interesting similarity shows up. A degree can be read as a society-curated certificate of knowledge and ability. Education puts what people consider frequent and useful into the curriculum, then uses exams and grades to confirm whether you have it. Its job has always been to help a person pick up a lot of previously organized knowledge in a shorter time.

Seen this way, degrees and AI share one functional similarity: both condense the past. A degree hands you "the past people consider worth learning"; AI recombines "the content and methods people have produced" for you.

Both are useful, and both share the same limit: neither can solve, on your behalf, the problems nobody has solved yet.

If what a person absorbs and builds up day to day already goes beyond what a degree was meant to prove, the degree's marginal value to them naturally drops. This is something I have observed lately. Degrees still have value; once a person's actual ability and experience exceed the range a degree represents, it simply tells you less.

So back to where this started. AI is like a modern elixir: it lets you quickly pick up what others spent years building. A degree is a condensation too, so are books, so are databases; AI just does this faster, at a larger scale, and more accessibly.

What moves the world forward is people taking those organized answers and doing the work, then finding halfway through: this part is wrong. So they stop, find the problem, and solve it. That solution becomes the next person's "past."

So new things only appear in execution. And the people who stay are the ones who can use AI to get the past quickly and are still willing to walk into the unknown themselves.
