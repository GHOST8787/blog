Three Variables of Happiness
18 Rules for Ages 20–25

Thinking Well-being Systems Thinking
2026.10.23 BY GHOST_1010

On his 27th birthday, the YouTuber 本子在隔壁 Benzi posted a 19-minute video that turns happiness into a formula: doing what you like, plus earning a decent amount of money, plus good relationships. The 18 rules that follow take the three variables apart one by one. This post records all 18, groups them by variable, and marks how they connect: which rule is the precondition for another, and which one corrects a side effect of the one before it.

# Why borrow from people ahead of you

The video opens on October 5, 2034, and asks what you look like then, what work you do, whether you are happy. Most people cannot answer. We overestimate how much we have changed in the past and underestimate how much we will change in the future, a pattern called the end-of-history illusion.

If you cannot picture the future, borrow it from someone who is further down the road. That is where the video starts, and where the 18 rules come from.

# Variable one: doing what you like

Rule 1 starts from a plain premise: nobody can force themselves to do something they dislike for very long. So the video runs the chain backwards. Change needs self-drive, self-drive needs passion, passion needs trying things, and trying needs curiosity. Curiosity is the fuel. Ask too many questions at school and teachers and parents push back, so some of it drains away in those years and has to be rebuilt slowly.

Rule 2 is about the rebuilding. The brain cannot grasp things outside what it already knows; it can only make connections next to existing knowledge. The author's own line ran from anime, to anime editing, to storyboarding and post-production, to character design, and finally into psychology. The first step is to take your current interests seriously, then reach a little further out.

Rule 3 adds another ingredient: boredom. In an experiment the video cites, people who first read a dull passage came up with more uses for a cup than people who had not. Empty time feeds imagination, and the urge to start making something.

Boredom has a downside too. An untrained mind fills the gap with self-doubt: how could I ever pull this off, I'll start tomorrow. Rule 4's answer is self-persuasion. Repeated setbacks turn into learned helplessness, like a baby elephant tied to a tree that, fully grown, no longer tries to break free. Getting past it means first convincing yourself that it might work out. What holds that belief up is a probability:

  • Per attempt20% chance of success
  • Attempts10
  • At least one89% (1 − 0.8¹⁰ ≈ 0.893)

Rule 5 explains why that one success is worth the cost. Citing The Formula by Albert-László Barabási, the video says early success is the single biggest factor in changing your trajectory; win once and you are more likely to win a second and third time. Every earlier attempt was groundwork for that one.

When trying does not get you through, rule 6 takes over: a problem that cannot be solved is part of the environment, and the move is to route around it. The author found he was a poor fit for vlogging (camera-shy, and not enough happening in his life to film), tried many times, and switched to explainer videos. Among the five designer mindsets in Designing Your Life (Bill Burnett and Dave Evans), this one is reframing the problem.

The "I control my life" mindset has a cost, and rule 7 accounts for it: believe you control your own life, and accept that other people may not control theirs. Research the video cites finds that the richer and more powerful people are, the harder they find it to understand others, and that feeling powerful lowers the activity of the mirroring system tied to empathy. Luck also weighs heavily. Most of the world's income gap comes down to which country you were born in. If you want the third term of the formula, good relationships, you have to drop the assumption that failure always comes from laziness.

Rule 8 is the cheapest correction tool: reading. The video quotes Charlie Munger and adds a precondition. Read about things you are already curious about, because knowledge with no link to your life does not stick; depth matters more than speed, quality more than quantity.

Read enough and books start contradicting each other, which is what rule 9 deals with. Most of reality sits in grey areas. A person can be good and bad at once; a relationship can nourish you and hurt you at the same time. Thinking in shades of grey means holding off on a verdict until you see the different sides of a thing.

# Variable two: earning a decent amount of money

Rule 10 defines the goal first: being rich is measured in freedom. If the goal is ever-upgrading consumption, every raise gets swallowed by bigger spending and more debt. The video's example is someone earning 500,000 a year who carries two mortgages and school fees for two children. That person has to keep working just to hold their current life together, and work spends the scarcest resource there is, time.

Rule 11 puts a number on freedom: passive income above expenses. Citing The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, the video defines wealth as assets that earn while you sleep; a salary does not count. FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early) draws a concrete line:

  • Target assetsAnnual spending × 25
  • Assumed return4% a year, enough to cover one year of spending
  • 5,000 / monthRequires 1.5 million (5,000 × 12 × 25)

The line is far away. The video's only comment: a target beats no target.

Rule 12 covers where passive income comes from: assets. Rich Dad Poor Dad defines an asset as anything that brings in cash flow. That includes a business that runs without you, stocks and bonds, rental property, royalties, and intangible assets such as a paid app or the videos on your channel. The video adds two caveats. Market conditions differ by country, so an asset's cash flow may not be steady. A home you live in produces no cash flow and comes with a mortgage, so it usually does not count as an asset.

For anyone who cannot afford property or pick stocks, rule 13 is something you can start on directly: be a producer. Making videos, writing, recording music and writing code all count, and the experience, systems and processes built along the way are intangible assets in themselves. A salary is stable and also caps your upside; production scales, so it has room for sudden growth.

Rule 14 adds a precondition to producing: serve others. Content made only for your own taste often finds no audience; what you produce has to offer knowledge, entertainment or emotional value. The video defines serving others as using your own abilities to influence and help people, and it pays the producer back with a sense of satisfaction.

Rule 15 is the insurance: keep a plan B outside your main work, and the further it sits from your job the better (the video cites Zhou Ling's book 認知驅動, roughly "Cognitive Drive"). When you are bored, you have something worthwhile to do. Your job and your hobby use different parts of the brain, so switching between them is rest in itself. And the two feed each other: the eye for design the author built through video work made him the person at the office whose diagrams look best.

# Variable three: good relationships

This part has only two rules, one that adds and one that subtracts.

Rule 16 adds: smile and listen. The video treats excessive pessimism about relationships as the start of a downward slide. Keep how you talk simple, smile more, listen more, and ask questions that actually land with people. In a fast-moving world, that is enough.

Rule 17 subtracts: keep away from people who keep putting you down. Someone who keeps telling you that you can't, and never offers support or recognition, does not belong in your circle however senior they are. This rule links back to rules 4 and 5. Success needs the self-efficacy of "I can," and self-persuasion runs on it; too much negativity breaks that switch, so the weight of other people's judgment needs clearing out on a regular basis.

# Rule 18 closes the loop

Rule 18 is to believe in kindness, and the video comes at it from the opposite side. On trial in Jerusalem, the Nazi official Adolf Eichmann was polite and insisted he was only a cog in the system. From this, Hannah Arendt described the banality of evil: evil comes from our own numbness. The everyday version is office gossip and sticking labels on people.

Numbness makes people cynical, and it drains their curiosity. At this point the video stops and asks: what was rule 1 again?

The answer is curiosity. Rule 18 connects back to rule 1, and the whole chain closes here: curiosity is the starting point, numbness wears it down, and believing in kindness is what keeps it alive.

The last segment switches to phone footage. The author says that two minutes earlier, on a video call, he had answered a doubting voice by saying he would make videos for the rest of his life, and then he cried; he describes it as his body "celebrating something beyond its control." The video ends on his 27th birthday.
