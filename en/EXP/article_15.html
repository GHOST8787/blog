Three Variables of Happiness
18 Rules for Ages 20–25

Thinking Well-being Systems Thinking
2026.10.23 BY GHOST_1010

On his 27th birthday, the YouTuber 本子在隔壁 Benzi posted a 19-minute video that turns happiness into a formula: happiness = doing what you like + earning a decent amount of money + good relationships, then breaks it down into 18 rules. This post records all 18, weaves them into three sections by variable, and marks how they connect: which rule is a precondition, which one corrects a side effect of the one before it, and why rule 18 loops back to rule 1.

# Why borrow from people ahead of you

The video opens on October 5, 2034, and asks what you look like then, what work you do, whether you are happy. Most people cannot answer. We overestimate how much we have changed in the past and underestimate how much we will change in the future, a pattern called the end-of-history illusion. If you cannot picture yourself ten years from now, look at people who have already walked that stretch of road. That is where the video starts: when you do not know who you will become, borrow from other people's lives first.

# Three threads through 18 rules

The 18 rules fall into three groups by variable. Rules 01 to 09 cover doing what you like and deal with how to find something you are willing to keep doing; rules 10 to 15 cover money and deal with what role money plays when what you want is freedom; rules 16 and 17 cover people and deal with how to get along with others while you build your own life; rule 18 ties it off and connects the whole chain back to rule 1. The groups interlock, and a later rule is often the precondition for an earlier one or a fix for its side effects.

# Variable one: doing what you like

The starting point is curiosity. Nobody can force themselves to do something they dislike for very long, so the video runs the chain backwards: change needs self-drive, self-drive needs passion, passion needs trying things, and trying needs curiosity. Run forwards, it reads curiosity → trying → passion → self-drive → change. Some of that curiosity drains away at school, where asking too many questions gets pushed back by teachers and parents. The way to rebuild it is to extend outward from what you already like: the brain can only make connections next to what it already knows, and the author's own line ran from anime, to anime editing, to storyboarding and post-production, to character design, and finally into psychology. Another ingredient is boredom. In an experiment the video cites, people who first read a dull passage came up with more uses for a cup.

Empty time also invites self-doubt: how could I ever pull this off, I'll start tomorrow. Repeated setbacks turn into learned helplessness, like a baby elephant tied to a tree that, fully grown, no longer tries to break free. The answer is continuous self-persuasion: first convince yourself that it might work out. What holds that belief up is a probability:

  • Per attempt20% chance of success
  • Attempts10
  • At least one89% (1 − 0.8¹⁰ ≈ 0.893)

One failure only stands for that one attempt. The success at the end is worth the cost, because success is the mother of success. Citing The Formula by Albert-László Barabási, the video says early success is the single biggest factor in changing your trajectory; win once and you are more likely to win a second and third time, and every earlier attempt was groundwork for it. A problem that trying cannot get through belongs to the environment, and the move is to route around it: the author is camera-shy and did not have enough happening in his life to film, tried vlogging many times, and switched to explainer videos. Among the five designer mindsets in Designing Your Life (Bill Burnett and Dave Evans), this one is reframing the problem.

The "I control my life" mindset needs one limit: believe you control your own life, and accept that other people may not control theirs. Research the video cites finds that feeling powerful lowers the activity of the mirroring system tied to empathy, and most of the world's income gap comes down to which country you were born in. If you want the third term of the formula, good relationships, you have to drop the assumption that failure always comes from laziness. The cheapest correction tool is reading. The video quotes Charlie Munger and adds a precondition: read about things you are already curious about, because knowledge with no link to your life does not stick; depth matters more than speed, quality more than quantity. Read enough and books start contradicting each other, so the last rule in this group is to embrace contradiction: a person can be good and bad at once, a relationship can nourish you and hurt you at the same time. Hold off on a verdict, stay curious, and let the answers contradict each other for a while.

# Variable two: earning a decent amount of money

The money group defines the goal first: being rich is measured in freedom. If rising income goes into ever-upgrading consumption, bigger spending and more debt swallow it. The video's example is someone earning 500,000 a year who carries two mortgages and school fees for two children; that person has to keep working just to hold their current life together, and work spends the scarcest resource there is, time. Freedom gets a number: passive income above expenses. The Almanack of Naval Ravikant defines wealth as assets that earn while you sleep. FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early) draws a concrete line:

  • Target assetsAnnual spending × 25
  • Assumed return4% a year, enough to cover one year of spending
  • 5,000 / monthRequires 1.5 million (5,000 × 12 × 25)

This is a simplified model that leaves out inflation, taxes and market swings. What it does is turn "I want to be free" into a target you can start calculating, and the video's only comment is that a target beats no target. Passive income comes from assets. Rich Dad Poor Dad defines an asset as anything that brings in cash flow: a business that runs without you, stocks and bonds, rental property, royalties, and intangible assets such as a paid app or the videos on your channel. Market conditions differ by country, so cash flow may not be steady, and a home you live in produces no cash flow and comes with a mortgage, so it usually does not count. Anyone who cannot afford property or pick stocks can be a producer right away. Making videos, writing, recording music and writing code all count, and the experience, systems and processes built along the way are intangible assets in themselves; a salary is stable and also caps your upside, while production scales and has room for sudden growth. Producing comes with a precondition, serving others: content made only for your own taste often finds no audience, so what you make has to offer knowledge, entertainment or emotional value. Here doing what you like and earning money connect for the first time: do what you are willing to keep doing, and solve other people's problems with it. The last rule is insurance: always keep a plan B, and the further it sits from your job the better (the video cites Zhou Ling's book 認知驅動, roughly "Cognitive Drive"). When you are bored, you have something worthwhile to do; your job and your hobby use different parts of the brain, so switching between them is rest in itself; and the two feed each other. The eye for design the author built through video work made him the person at the office whose diagrams look best, which makes plan B a second growth curve alongside the main job.

# Variable three: good relationships

The people group has one rule that adds and one that subtracts. The addition is to smile and listen: the video treats excessive pessimism about relationships as the start of a downward slide, and keeping how you talk simple, smiling more, listening more and asking questions that land is enough in a fast-moving world, as long as the other person can tell you are really listening. The subtraction is to keep away from people who keep putting you down. Someone who keeps telling you that you can't, and never offers support or recognition, does not belong in your circle however senior they are. This links back to self-persuasion: success needs the self-efficacy of "I can," too much negativity breaks that switch, and so the weight of other people's judgment needs clearing out on a regular basis. Relationships also include deciding which people get to shape you over the long run.

# Rule 18 closes the loop

Rule 18 is to believe in kindness, and the video comes at it from the opposite side. On trial in Jerusalem, the Nazi official Adolf Eichmann was polite and insisted he was only a cog in the system. From this, Hannah Arendt described the banality of evil: evil comes from our own numbness. The everyday version is office gossip and sticking labels on people. Numbness, over time, makes people cynical and drains their curiosity, and at this point the video stops and asks: what was rule 1 again? The answer is curiosity. Go numb and you stop asking questions; stop asking and you stop trying to understand; curiosity wears away with it. Believing in kindness is what keeps rule 1 alive, and the whole chain closes here. The last segment switches to phone footage. The author says that two minutes earlier, on a video call, he had answered a doubting voice by saying he would make videos for the rest of his life, and then he cried; he describes it as his body "celebrating something beyond its control." The video ends on his 27th birthday.

# The loop the 18 rules form

Put together, the 18 rules form one loop: curiosity → trying → failing → adjusting → succeeding → producing → creating value → gaining more freedom → keeping good relationships → holding on to kindness → staying awake to the world → back to curiosity. The three variables pull on each other inside it: passion shapes how you learn and how you work; money buys you choices; the people around you feed back into your self-efficacy, your curiosity and your drive to act.

# Why I recommend the video

None of this conflicts much with how I have understood the world up to now. I recommend watching it; the video's storytelling and logic are very good, and it has a lot of substance. In under 20 minutes it puts passion, money and relationships into a single framework and shows how each rule relates to the ones around it. For someone between 20 and 25, that framework is more useful than memorising any single rule for success: before you know who you will be in ten years, you can look at how someone a little further down the road describes the world they see.
